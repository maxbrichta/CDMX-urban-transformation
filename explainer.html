Mexico City · Urban Transformation

What each view of the map shows

The map has five views. Each measures a different trace of the rearrangement of a few central colonias (neighborhoods) around remote workers and foreigners who coordinate their social/economic lives around platforms.

The social infrastructure Venues hosting internationally-oriented events, plus co-working, co-living and hostels

What's on the map

Venues in the dataset that have hosted internationally-oriented events, such as language exchanges, run clubs, founder meetups, parties, speed dating, and other social gatherings. Also shows known co-working spaces, co-livings, and hostels. The dot size reflects how many events that venue has held, so a café running a weekly event grows into a hub while a one-off venue stays small.

What it says about platformization

This social substratum is visible because it is platform-mediated. Events are advertised and discovered on Meetup, Eventbrite, Instagram and WhatsApp. According to interviews with digital nomads and expats, this social event network has grown drastically since the pandemic, when the city saw an influx of foreigners. Platforms shape the local economy by bringing these social networks into physical locations that economically benefit from hosting them.

How to read it

Look at concentration rather than individual dots. The circuit does not spread evenly across the city. It clusters around the Roma, Condesa, and Juárez colonias, and keeps returning to the same addresses. Some of the locations host events from multiple networks.

How the data is gathered

The data primarily comes from weekly postings on Eventbrite and Meetup. Additional data is purposively scraped from organizer Instagram accounts using the open source tool Zeeschuimer. Events are selected for their international audience orientation, either because they are posted in English or because they specifically are advertised to foreigners.

Data limitations

There are several networks that do not publicly post the locations of their events, such as Fight Club, and other events that are posted on Luma, which is not accessible for automated scraping. Data collection started in October 2026.

Where the Airbnbs are Short-let listings per km², by postal code, across quarterly scrapes

What's on the map

Short-let listings per square kilometer in each postal code, from Inside Airbnb. The slider steps through quarterly scrapes to show the changes in listings throughout the year.

What it says about platformization

As a platform Airbnb takes housing built and regulated as housing and re-prices it as a nightly product. The densest postal codes (Hipódromo, Condesa, Roma Norte) are the same blocks the social event circuit is primarily based. The overlap demonstrates acute impact of platforms on these neighborhoods.

How to read it

The lighter shadings (on dark mode) reflect areas with the most density of listings. Use the slider to compare quarters. Read the jump between March and June 2026 as an event rather than a trend: the city hosted World Cup matches, and listings rose about 38%.

Limitations

A listing is not a dwelling. Hosts run several, some are single rooms rather than whole units, and duplicates are present in the data; the entire-home count is the closer proxy. The data are scraped, so coverage reflects what was publicly visible that day. And the series begins in late 2025, well after the pandemic-era rise, so this layer shows the recent state of the market rather than how it got there.

What changed in commerce Cafés gained or lost between the 2019 and 2025 business registers

What's on the map

The change in the number of cafés in each postal code between the November 2019 and May 2025 editions of INEGI's national business register, the DENUE. The slider shows changes from year-to-year.

What it says about platformization

Cafés are the physical plant of platform-mediated work. Remote workers need somewhere to sit with wifi, and in Mexico City cafés absorb some of that demand. Economists studying neighborhood change have argued that the composition of local commerce moves before rents and demographics do (Glaeser, Kim and Luca 2018), which makes this the earliest visible signal on the map. They call it a leading indicator, while being explicit that it predicts change rather than causing it: businesses respond to a shifting neighborhood at least as much as they drive one.

How to read it

The color runs either side of zero: warm where cafés were added, cool where they were lost, pale in the middle. At the left-hand end of the slider the baseline is being compared with itself, so every polygon reads zero. Switch the measure to a level, such as cafés per km², to read a single edition on its own.

Limitations

The DENUE is a directory INEGI re-canvasses, not a register of openings and closings: a net change of +20 does not mean twenty cafés opened, it means twenty more were found. It undercounts informal commerce, which biases the comparison against street and market trade far harder than against cafés. And INEGI did not substantially re-canvass Mexico City between 2021 and 2023, so the middle of the series is flat as a consequence.

Where buildings were renewed Share of built surface dated 2010 or later in the city cadastre (the city's official list of land and buildings)

What's on the map

The share of each postal code's built surface whose cadastral record is dated 2010 or later, from the city's 2021 catastro (list of buildings, where they sit, its boundaries, how big it is, what's built on it, and what the state assesses it to be worth).

What it says about platformization

Where a unit earns more by the night than by the month, renovation and conversion follow. The cadastre records these new builds, renovations, and floors added.

How to read it

This counts floor space, not buildings. One big new development changes an area's color more than a dozen small remodels do.

Limitations

The field records construction or the last remodel, which is what makes it a renovation signal but makes it useless as a map of building age (i.e. a 1920s house renovated in 2015 reads as 2015). The snapshot is from 2021, so the most recent wave is not included. Land values in this layer run below market and are revalued on the city's own schedule, so read them as relative, not as current prices.

What businesses are findable on social media Facebook and Instagram pages per business on the official register

What's on the map

Business pages on Facebook and Instagram, counted against the businesses on the official register. The source is Overture Maps, which in Mexico City is 94% Meta data. Further measures break the same ratio down by trade: food and drink, retail, health care, sport.

What it says about platformization

This measures platformization itself rather than one of its effects. Someone who arrived three months ago finds places through Instagram and Google Maps. A business that is not there is, for that customer, not there at all — however long it has stood on the corner. The layer shows where the city has been made legible to that way of looking, and where it has not.

How to read it

It is a ratio between two different counts, not a percentage, and it can exceed 1. A low score means invisible to the apps, not empty of commerce — frequently the opposite. Read the per-trade measures alongside the overall one, because they disagree: Doctores scores 0.44 overall and 1.73 on health care, because it is the hospital district and clinics market themselves on Facebook. The headline figure hides that entirely.

Limitations

Meta pages include duplicates, home-based sellers and entries that are not businesses; the official register undercounts informal commerce. There is also no time dimension. The records carry no creation date, so this layer can show the current state and not the change.

Three things that apply to every layer

  • The shading is by postal code, not colonia. Postal codes are a postal convenience and they cut across the neighborhood boundaries people actually use. A polygon named Roma Norte is the postal code that mostly covers it.
  • The layers are not a time series. They were measured in different years with different instruments (a 2021 cadastre, a 2025 business register, a 2026 scrape). Reading them against one another is useful, but be cognizant of the disjointedness in timescales.
  • Correlation here is geographic, not causal. Cafés, listings and renovations concentrate in the same few colonias because those colonias were already central, well-served and desirable. Separating what platforms did from what was already underway is the work the map sets up, but doesn't finish.
